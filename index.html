Banging Before Hammering:
Developmentally Inspired Pretraining for Robot Skill Learning

Abstract

Before children use a hammer, infants repeatedly bang handheld objects against surfaces, practicing a proposed precursor to percussive tool use. We ask two linked questions: what may sustain such apparently goal-free banging, and can an analogous developmental progression improve robot learning? We hypothesize that self-generated auditory and haptic feedback provides intrinsic reinforcement. In a controlled study, ten infants aged 7–14 months explored objects and surfaces of different hardness. Infants banged the hard object more on the hard than on the soft surface, consistent with sensory reinforcement but not separating its effects from contact mechanics. We then train a simulated Franka to bang using an impact-force reward and compare three approaches to targeted hammering: PPO from scratch, fine-tuning the banging policy, and hierarchical reuse of the frozen skill with Gaussian Process Upper Confidence Bound (GP-UCB) selecting its initial location. Fine-tuning accelerates early learning while attaining final success comparable to training from scratch. Hierarchical reuse achieves high success earlier, but reaches lower final success and requires more impacts to hit the target under the evaluated protocol. These results reveal a tradeoff between rapidly deploying an acquired skill and refining its targeted execution. We will release synchronized motion-capture, audio, and RGB-D recordings from the infant study. Together, these studies show how developmental observations can suggest what robots should practice, while robot experiments test how acquired behaviors can be reused.
